\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Asset Universe}
\label{subsec:data_assets}

The equity universe consists of ten large-cap Brazilian stocks listed on the B3
exchange: PETR4, VALE3, ITUB4, BBDC4, ABEV3, WEGE3, RENT3, SUZB3, B3SA3, and BBAS3.
These ten tickers were selected based on three criteria: (i)~continuous listing
throughout the full sample period January~2015 to December~2024, (ii)~membership in
the Ibovespa index for at least eight of the ten years, and (iii)~average daily
traded volume exceeding R\$~100 million, ensuring that the portfolio weights implied
by the optimization are implementable without significant market-impact costs.
The ten assets span six sectors---energy and materials (PETR4, VALE3, SUZB3),
financials (ITUB4, BBDC4, BBAS3, B3SA3), consumer staples (ABEV3), industrials
(WEGE3), and services (RENT3)---providing cross-sector diversification within the
concentrated structure of the Brazilian equity market.

Daily adjusted closing prices were obtained from Yahoo Finance via the
\texttt{yfinance} library.
Log returns were computed as $r_{i,t} = \log(P_{i,t}/P_{i,t-1})$ for each asset~$i$
and trading day~$t$.
The full sample spans January~2015 to December~2024, comprising 2,487 trading days.
Table~\ref{tab:descriptive_stats} reports summary statistics for log returns over
the full period.
The cross-sectional dispersion in annualized returns is substantial: WEGE3 delivers
the highest annualized return at 23.9\%, followed by PETR4 at 26.0\%, while ABEV3
records the lowest at 1.2\%, reflecting both sector dynamics and firm-specific factors.
The equal-weight portfolio achieves a mean daily return of $0.000545$ (annualized:
$\approx13.7\%$) with a daily standard deviation of $0.015521$ and a
CVaR$_{0.95}$ of $-0.0249$.

\subsection{Macroeconomic Variables}
\label{subsec:data_macro}

Two macroeconomic series enter the state vector, both retrieved from the Banco
Central do Brasil public time-series API (SGS, \texttt{api.bcb.gov.br}).
A third series, IPCA inflation (SGS~433), is collected and stored but is not used
by any model reported here.

\textbf{SELIC target rate (SGS~432).}
The policy rate set by the Monetary Policy Committee (COPOM), in percent per
annum.
This is the target rate rather than the realised overnight rate (SGS~11): the
target is the instrument the committee actually decides on, is piecewise constant
between meetings, and therefore gives an unambiguous reading of the policy stance
on any given day.
It enters the state vector as a rolling 252-day z-score, which removes the level
drift and leaves the variable comparable across the sample.

\textbf{Bank lending spread (SGS~20786).}
The average spread on non-earmarked credit operations to households, in
percentage points, that is, the difference between the average rate charged by
banks on such operations and their cost of funding.
It enters the state vector as a rolling 252-day z-score.
This is a domestic bank-credit measure, not a corporate-bond-versus-government
spread, and it captures tightening in the transmission of monetary policy to
household credit rather than sovereign or corporate default risk.

We note explicitly what is \emph{not} in the state vector, because both are
natural candidates that a reader may expect: there is no sovereign CDS series and
no implied-volatility index.
Neither was available to this study at daily frequency over the full sample
without a paid data subscription, and no proxy for either is used.
The macro block of the state vector is therefore two scalars plus the regime
encoding of Section~\ref{subsec:data_regimes}, and any claim this paper makes
about ``macro conditioning'' refers to that and nothing more.

Both series are aligned to the equity trading calendar by forward fill, since
neither reports on every trading day.

\subsection{SELIC Regime Classification}
\label{subsec:data_regimes}

Monetary-policy regimes are identified from the direction of the SELIC target
using a rule-based classification over a trailing quarter.
Let $\Delta_{63}(t)$ be the change in the SELIC target over the previous 63
trading days.
The regime at date $t$ is labeled \emph{Rising} if
$\Delta_{63}(t) > +0.25$ percentage points, \emph{Falling} if
$\Delta_{63}(t) < -0.25$, and \emph{Stable} otherwise.
The quarter-long window rather than the single most recent COPOM decision is
deliberate: the committee meets eight times a year and often moves in a sequence
of same-signed steps, so a one-meeting rule would flip the label during pauses
inside an otherwise continuous cycle.
The $\pm 0.25$ threshold is one standard policy increment, which keeps a single
minimum-size move from being read as a change of regime.
The classification is strictly backward-looking---only information available at
each date enters it---so it introduces no look-ahead bias.
Table~\ref{tab:regime_stats} summarizes the regime distribution across the
2,487-day sample.

Over the full period, the Stable regime accounts for the majority of trading days
(42.1\%, or 1,048 days), reflecting the extended periods between rate decisions.
The Rising regime covers 23.5\% of the sample (584 days), concentrated primarily
in 2015--2016 and 2021--2023.
The Falling regime accounts for the remaining 34.4\% (855 days), corresponding
to the easing cycle of 2017--2020 and the initial phase of the 2023--2024
normalization.

The economic significance of these regimes is confirmed by the large variation in
aggregate Sharpe ratios: the equal-weight portfolio achieves a Sharpe ratio of
$-0.23$ during Rising-rate periods, $+0.88$ during Stable periods, and $+0.62$
during Falling periods.
This three-way split provides a natural test of whether a regime-aware portfolio
strategy can exploit this heterogeneity to improve risk-adjusted performance.

\subsection{Stationarity}
\label{subsec:data_stationarity}

All ten log-return series were tested for stationarity using the Augmented
Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test.
All ten tickers reject the unit-root null hypothesis at the 1\% significance level
($p < 0.001$), confirming that log returns are stationary in levels.
This is consistent with standard results in the empirical finance literature and
validates the use of log returns as the primary input to the model without
additional differencing.
