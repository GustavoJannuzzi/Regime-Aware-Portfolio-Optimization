\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Economic Interpretation of the CVaR Reward}
\label{subsec:disc_cvar}

The motivating argument for a CVaR objective is sound and we do not retract it.
Brazilian equities exhibit pronounced negative skewness and excess kurtosis
relative to developed-market benchmarks, driven by periodic currency crises,
commodity price shocks, and fiscal uncertainty.
The Sharpe ratio, treating upside and downside deviations symmetrically, values a
large positive outlier as much as it penalizes an equal negative one, which is not
how a risk-averse investor evaluates outcomes.

The implementation tested here, however, does not deliver that objective, and the
ablation makes the failure visible rather than hiding it.
Moving from A2 to A3---the single change being the reward---degrades both the
efficiency and the tail behaviour of the resulting portfolios.
This is not the direction the motivating argument predicts, and the explanation is
in the construction of the reward rather than in the market.

Consider how the reward at date $t$ is computed (Section~\ref{subsec:mdp}).
The portfolio return series entering the CVaR estimate is
$\mathbf{r}_{t-T:t}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_t$ with $T = 20$: the candidate weights are
scored against the \emph{preceding} twenty trading days.
The state at the same date, by construction, contains the normalized returns of
those same twenty days.
The reward is therefore a deterministic function of the state and the action, and
contributes no information that the agent does not already observe.
There is no environment feedback in the usual sense: maximizing this signal is not
a decision problem about the future but a re-scoring of data the agent has already
seen.
Under this objective the optimal policy is the one that minimizes the trailing
twenty-day tail loss of the observed window, which makes the agent a rolling
short-window tail-risk optimizer.
A twenty-day estimate of a $95\%$ tail is built on one observation, and the
out-of-sample behaviour of A3 is what one would expect of a portfolio rule fitted
to that.

The contrast with A1 and A2 is instructive precisely because it is structural.
Their reward is the realized portfolio return on day $t$ under the behavioral
action, a quantity that is absent from the state and only becomes known after the
action is fixed.
That is genuine feedback, and it is the configuration in which the conservatism
penalty produces its clearest gain.
The ablation, read this way, does not show that tail-risk-aware objectives fail in
the Brazilian market.
It shows that a reward which is measurable from the state alone cannot teach an
agent anything, whatever quantity it is named after.

The correction is well defined and is the first item of future work: score the
action against the returns that follow it, estimating the tail over a forward
window $\mathbf{r}_{t:t+T}$, or over a longer trailing window that is deliberately
excluded from the state.
Until that experiment is run, no claim in this paper about CVaR reward shaping
should be carried forward, in either direction.
Note also that $\lambda = 0.5$ was fixed a priori and never tuned; the grid search
of Section~\ref{subsec:cql} varied only the conservative weight.

\subsection{Role of Macroeconomic State Variables}
\label{subsec:disc_macro}

Adding the macro block lifts Sharpe from 0.2111 to 0.4058 while leaving tail loss
and drawdown worse than in either Sharpe-reward configuration.
The gain is real in the arithmetic and difficult to attribute, for two reasons that
compound.

The first is the reward.
A3 and A4 share an objective that, as argued in Section~\ref{subsec:disc_cvar},
supplies no feedback: it is computable from the state.
Adding five dimensions to a state under such an objective changes which
trailing-window portfolio the optimizer settles on, and that change can raise or
lower out-of-sample Sharpe without the new dimensions carrying information about
the allocation decision at all.
The comparison that would separate the two accounts, A3 against A4 under a reward
scored on returns the agent has not yet seen, has not been run.

The second is the regime-conditional evidence, which points against the mechanism
the hypothesis proposes.
If the macro block were informative about tightening cycles specifically, the
agent's advantage should concentrate in the rising-rate regime.
It is inverted: the agent trails the naive benchmark most widely precisely there
(0.2811 against 1.2050) and leads only while rates fall (0.4609 against
$-0.4524$).
That pattern is what a persistently defensive allocation produces mechanically,
and it does not require the agent to have learned anything about monetary policy.
A beta-matched comparison would test this directly and is not part of this study.

One methodological point survives intact and is worth stating, because it is the
part of the original argument that the data does not touch: both macro inputs are
observable in real time and require no forecast or latent-state estimate, so an
agent conditioned on them is implementable without a nowcasting apparatus.
That property is a precondition for the approach, not evidence for it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:disc_limitations}

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.
The first three are, in our view, the ones that most constrain how the results
of Section~\ref{sec:results} may be read.

\textbf{No measure of uncertainty.}
Every number reported in this paper comes from a single evaluation window and a
single random seed.
We provide no confidence interval on any Sharpe ratio and no test of the
difference between any two strategies.
With 720 trading days of out-of-sample data and a training procedure whose
outcome depends on initialisation, differences of the magnitude reported here
are well within the range that seed variation alone can produce in offline RL.
The ordering of the configurations should therefore be read as a description of
this run, not as an estimate of a population quantity.
Repeating the ablation across seeds and reporting the dispersion is the single
most valuable extension of this work, and it is not expensive: the whole ladder
runs in under two hours on a laptop CPU.

\textbf{Asymmetric evaluation protocols.}
As set out in Section~\ref{subsec:walkforward}, the benchmarks re-estimate their
weights every quarter while the agent is trained once and then frozen for three
years.
The comparison is not like-for-like, and the direction of the bias is against
the agent.
A per-fold retrained agent would settle this, and this study does not provide one.

\textbf{A thin macro block.}
The macro information available to the agent is two scalars and a three-way
regime encoding (Section~\ref{subsec:data_macro}).
Sovereign credit risk and implied volatility, the two variables most often cited
as carrying emerging-market stress, are absent.
Any conclusion about the value of ``macro conditioning'' is therefore a
conclusion about this particular thin encoding of the policy stance, and says
little about a richer one.

\textbf{Transaction costs, and why they are decisive here.}
The evaluation protocol excludes transaction costs, and this is the assumption
on which the reported comparison rests.
The agent does not rebalance quarterly.
The evaluation applies the policy's predicted weights on every trading day, so
the agent rebalances daily, while the benchmarks hold their weights fixed
between quarterly re-estimations.
Measuring one-way turnover as
$\tau_t = \sum_i \lvert w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1} \rvert$ by inference over the
evaluation set gives annualised turnover of 38.5$\times$ portfolio value for
A1 and 87.9$\times$ for A2, against approximately 1.0$\times$ for the
Minimum-Variance Portfolio, 0.5$\times$ for Risk Parity, and zero for
Equal-Weight, which never trades.

At a round-trip cost of 10 basis points---a conservative figure for liquid B3
names once brokerage, exchange fees, and slippage are included---this
corresponds to roughly 3.9\% to 8.8\% per year for the agents and about
0.1\% per year for the benchmarks.
Annualised returns of the agents in the evaluation window are of the order of
9\%.
A cost differential of that size does not merely reduce the magnitude of any
outperformance: it is of the same order as the entire return being compared.
No conclusion in Section~\ref{sec:results} should be read as a statement about
implementable performance, and any future version of this study must either
constrain turnover in the reward or report results net of a cost model.

A related observation concerns concentration.
The mean maximum weight assigned by the full agent is 0.58, and on 525 of the
748 evaluation days a single asset exceeds half of the portfolio.
The conservatism penalty of CQL bounds the agent to actions supported by the
behavioral data; it does not, and is not designed to, impose diversification.

\textbf{No short selling.}
The simplex constraint (Equation~\eqref{eq:action_space}) restricts the agent to
long-only positions.
This is consistent with regulatory constraints on many Brazilian retail and
institutional funds but prevents the agent from hedging systematic risk by taking
short positions in overvalued assets or index futures.
Extending the framework to a box-constrained action space would be a natural
next step.

\textbf{Regime shift risk.}
The regime classification is based on COPOM decisions, which are discrete events
occurring eight times per year.
In periods of rapid or unprecedented monetary-policy changes---such as the
emergency rate cuts during the COVID-19 pandemic---the regime label may lag
the true economic environment by several weeks.
More sophisticated regime-detection methods, such as hidden Markov models or
real-time macro nowcasting, could reduce this lag.

\textbf{Asset universe concentration.}
The ten-asset universe, while representative of the B3 blue-chip segment, is
highly concentrated in financials and commodity-linked sectors.
Expanding the universe to include small- and mid-cap stocks, fixed-income
instruments, or real-estate investment trusts (FIIs) would test the
generalizability of the proposed framework.

\subsection{Practical Implications}
\label{subsec:disc_implications}

We see no basis in these results for recommending the proposed strategy to a
Brazilian fund manager, and three findings argue specifically against it.
The agent is dominated by an equal-weight portfolio on every metric measured; it
rebalances daily, at a turnover that would carry costs of the same order as its
entire return; and it concentrates more than half of the portfolio in a single
asset on 525 of 748 evaluation days.
Any of the three would be disqualifying on its own.

What the study does offer a practitioner is narrower and, we think, more durable.
The offline formulation itself holds up: training on a fixed historical dataset,
with a conservatism penalty bounding the policy to actions the data supports,
produced the single clearest improvement in the ablation and requires no market
simulator whose realism would have to be defended to an auditor.
That property makes the framework auditable in a way online RL in a simulated
market is not, and it is independent of whether this particular agent works.

The finding most likely to save someone else's time is the reward specification.
An objective that can be computed from the observation is not a reward, however it
is labelled, and in a financial MDP the mistake is easy to make: trailing-window
risk statistics are natural quantities to optimize and natural quantities to put
in a state vector, and putting the same window in both places is silent.
Checking that the reward depends on data the agent has not yet observed costs
nothing and would have caught this before the experiment ran.
